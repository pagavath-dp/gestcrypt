# Discussion of the Exploratory Pilot

## Acceptance and repeatability trade-off

The observed signature acceptance fraction fell as the ambiguity margin increased: **21/24 (87.5%)** with loose margins, **14/24 (58.3%)** with baseline margins, and **5/24 (20.8%)** with strict margins. Rejection rose correspondingly from **3/24 (12.5%)** to **10/24 (41.7%)** and **19/24 (79.2%)**. This is consistent with wider guard bands excluding more measurements near fixed quantization thresholds. It does not by itself show that either guard band is appropriately calibrated: the export contains outcome codes, not the feature values needed to inspect boundary distances or instability.

Among accepted genuine trials with an accepted reference, loose and baseline each matched in **85.7%** of comparisons (**18/21** and **12/14**, respectively). Strict matched **3/3**, but that denominator is too small to support a claim of superior repeatability. Only **5/24** strict trials produced signatures, and only **1/7** strict reference attempts was accepted. Two of the five accepted strict trials had no accepted reference, leaving three comparable accepted trials. Moreover, **17** rejected strict trials also lacked a reference. The strict configuration therefore made many interactions unavailable before a match decision could be reached. Its end-to-end match fraction of **3/5 (60.0%)** applies only to the five trials with an available reference, not to all 24 trials.

Loose margins gave the highest observed acceptance (**21/24**) and end-to-end matching with reference available (**18/24**). They also yielded **3 accepted mismatches** overall, all in fist trials. Higher acceptance alone cannot establish an optimal setting. This pilot contains no different-pose or second-person attempts, so it cannot assess whether looser margins would also increase unintended cross-gesture matches. The small within-session pose set does not justify changing the production baseline. A margin choice should be documented from pilot evidence, frozen before held-out collection, and judged there against both availability and erroneous matches.

## Pose and reference effects

The four poses did not behave uniformly. Under baseline margins, fist produced **1 match, 2 mismatches, and 3 rejections among 6 genuine trials**. Open palm produced **4 matches and 3 rejections among 7**; three-finger produced **3 matches and 3 rejections among 6**; SWAG produced **4 matches and 1 rejection among 5**. Loose margins removed all three-finger and SWAG trial rejections in this session, while fist still produced **3 mismatches among 6 trials**. These observations suggest that feature boundaries and pose execution interact, but the data cannot isolate the cause. In particular, a mismatch could reflect natural hand variation, a feature definition, a reference choice, or positioning changes.

Reference acceptance determines whether trial comparisons are even available. Three-finger had **three recorded reference attempts** in the pilot and baseline accepted only **1/3**; strict accepted **0/3**. SWAG had **two reference attempts**, of which strict accepted **1/2**. A new reference can change the candidate used by subsequent trials, so an overall per-pose match rate may mix comparisons against different reference captures. Schema v1 retains each trial's candidate `reference_id`, but lacks the later reference-group and retry-predecessor fields. The pilot's repeated references should not be reconstructed as formally indexed retries.

## Timing and design implications

The recorded **829 ms median** for both attempt and capture latency among **31 attempts** is an end-to-end observation for this one camera and session. The capture timer includes readiness waiting; the attempt timer can additionally include camera initialization. Neither measures isolated MediaPipe inference, rendering, or cryptographic work. More devices, lighting conditions, and rejected attempts would be needed before making responsiveness claims.

For GestCrypt, these findings concern the reliability of **gesture-assisted interaction**. They do not establish gesture-based identity verification. A person who can reproduce a gesture may produce the same signature, and this pilot did not test impostors, liveness, replay, or offline guessing. New v2 files rely on a mandatory passphrase and random per-file data key; gesture test-mode outcomes do not change v2 key strength. Legacy v1 remains a separate gesture-derived path with different credential risks. A practical design should report capture rejection clearly and retain camera-free v2 recovery while evaluating gesture reliability on held-out, consented attempts.
